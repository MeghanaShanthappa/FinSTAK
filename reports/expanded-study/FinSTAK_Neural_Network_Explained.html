Inside FinSTAK:
from market history to a prediction.

FinSTAK looks at recent prices and available company events, predicts the next trading day’s prices, and learns by comparing its predictions with historical outcomes.

Based on the 100-stock, 2014–2023 experiment · 27,030 source-checked event records · Prepared 22 November 2026

01 · The taskWhat are we asking the network to predict?

For each company, we predict the next trading session’s open, high, low and close, usually shortened to OHLC.

PriceMeaning
OpenThe first trading price of the session.
HighThe highest price during the session.
LowThe lowest price during the session.
CloseThe final trading price of the session.

Imagine a stock closes today at $100. We ask: “Using information available by today’s cutoff, what will tomorrow’s four prices be?” This is an illustrative example, not a saved forecast.

During training, tomorrow has already happened in our historical dataset. We hide tomorrow’s observed prices while making the prediction, then reveal them to calculate the error.

A neural network learns adjustable numbers called weights. Training repeatedly changes these weights to reduce historical errors. An embedding is a list of numbers representing something, such as a company or a piece of text.

02 · The architectureTwo information sources, one forecast.

The reference configuration is FinSTAK without learned decay. Other experiments switch components on, replace them, or remove them. We did not put every experimental technique into the reference model.

03 · The numerical inputsHow do prices become an embedding?

Each stock supplies 19 measurements per session.

GroupNumberContents
Normalized prices4Open, high, low and close.
Normalized volume1Log-transformed trading volume, scaled separately.
Derived features14Returns, candle body, range, wick sizes, volume change, recent variability, position relative to a short moving average, and history-availability flags.

First, make the scales comparable

A $20 stock and a $500 stock have different raw price levels. We normalize OHLC using shared price statistics from the trailing 90 observed sessions. Volume has separate normalization. Remaining feature scalers are fitted using training-period data.

Then, learn useful combinations

A linear layer transforms the day’s 19 features into 32 numbers. It learns weighted combinations of the input measurements. We add a separate, learned 32-number company embedding identifying which company the observation belongs to.

19 features→32 numbers+ company embedding

The company embedding begins with random values and changes during training. It is not a company description supplied by a language model, and its individual coordinates do not have predefined meanings.

04 · Sequence memoryWhat does the GRU do?

A Gated Recurrent Unit, or GRU, processes a sequence in order. It reads one session, updates its memory, then reads the next.

Session 1→Session 2→ … →Session 30→32-number summary

Its gates act like adjustable valves. They learn how much previous information to retain and how much new information to incorporate. The model may learn that a recent large movement matters more than an older small movement; we do not manually tell it which pattern to remember.

The same GRU weights are shared across all 100 companies. We do not train 100 separate GRUs. Each company has its own input sequence and company embedding.

Memory is recomputed for each historical window. The GRU does not carry an unlimited memory across all ten years. In this implementation it summarizes market history, rather than a recurrent history of past event-graph states.

05 · Text embeddingsHow does a sentence become 768 numbers?

Consider an illustrative event: “Company A announces an acquisition.” The embedding input includes the event description and its supporting source quotation.

  1. Tokenize. Split the text into smaller pieces called tokens.
  2. Process the context. The pretrained text model calculates a representation for each token while considering surrounding text.
  3. Pool. Average the token representations, ignoring artificial padding used to make batches equal in length.
  4. Normalize vector length. Scale the resulting list so its mathematical length is one.
  5. Cache the result. Save a 768-number embedding so we can reuse it during training.

The reference text encoder is EmbeddingGemma 2. Its input is prefixed with the recorded classification-task instruction. Text beyond its configured limit is rejected rather than silently cut off.

The 768 coordinates are not named financial indicators. Coordinate 12 does not automatically mean “acquisition strength.” Related texts may receive related representations, but their forecasting usefulness still needs to be learned and tested.

Frozen means unchanged during FinSTAK training. The pretrained text encoder produces the cached vectors before forecasting training. FinSTAK does not update the encoder’s weights each time a price prediction is wrong.

How do 768 numbers interact with 32 stock numbers?

A trainable projection transforms 768 → 32. We add a learned 32-number category embedding—for example, earnings or litigation. These transformations adapt the fixed text representation to the forecasting task.

RepresentationSizeWhat changes during FinSTAK training?
Cached event-text vector768It stays fixed.
Projected event representation32The projection weights learn.
Company identity embedding32The embedding values learn.
Event-category embedding32The embedding values learn.
Stock-history summary32The GRU and input transformations learn.

Matching vector sizes allows combination. It does not automatically give the vectors identical meanings; training learns useful relationships between them.

What changes in the FinBERT experiment?

We replace the frozen text encoder with FinBERT while keeping event records and routing arrays matched. Long texts are split into chunks, their token representations are pooled across chunks, and the output vector is normalized. FinBERT is also frozen during forecasting training.

06 · Choosing useful informationWhat is attention?

Imagine answering a question while looking through several reference notes. Attention learns which notes deserve more weight for that question.

Attention termRole in FinSTAKAnalogy
QueryA learned transformation of the stock-history summary.The question being asked.
KeyA learned transformation of an eligible event representation.The information used to judge whether a note is relevant.
ValueAnother learned transformation of that event representation.The information the note contributes.

The network compares queries and keys, calculates relevance scores, and converts the scores into nonnegative attention weights. It then forms a weighted combination of the value vectors.

There are four attention heads: four parallel learned scoring systems, each operating on eight dimensions of the 32-dimensional representation. We do not prescribe a meaning such as “earnings specialist” for a particular head.

A learned null option allows the model to put weight on contributing no event message. It is not forced to treat one of the available events as useful.

The resulting 32-number event contribution is called a shock in the code. Here, “shock” means a learned contribution—not a demonstrated causal effect.

Attention is not a probability that the news is true. All 27,030 exported event-confidence fields in this expanded study are null. Event attention uses unit source weights; calibrated confidence-weighted event edges were not tested.

07 · Correlations and graph connectionsWhat is correlated with what?

RelationshipMeaningHow we use it
Text similarityTwo texts have related meanings.Represented by the text encoder; not used to construct this company graph.
Stock-return correlationTwo companies’ returns have tended to move together.Selects company-to-company connections.
Learned attention relevanceAn event representation appears useful for a stock’s forecast.Learned from the forecasting loss.
The company graph is built from stock-return correlation, not embedding similarity. Correlation does not establish that one company causes another to move.

At each decision date, we calculate correlations using the previous 60 observed log returns. Each stock receives up to three positive-correlation peers, retaining connections with correlation of at least 0.2.

An event first enters its explicitly identified company. The graph can then pass that company’s event contribution to related companies. Peer attention combines learned relevance with the correlation weight.

In the reference model, peer links carry event contributions, rather than entire stock-history representations. The T-GCN-style experimental control uses a different price-information route.

Final stock representation = local history summary
+ direct event contribution
+ peer event contribution
When is an event eligible, and does it eventually expire?

The recorded decision cutoff is 23:59:59 UTC on a trading session. News becomes eligible two calendar days after its reported publication time—an assumed conservative delay, not a verified historical arrival time. SEC availability follows the later filing/UTC acceptance date. Category age limits allow up to 90 sessions for earnings, 500 for litigation and 15 for other categories, subject to explicit validity boundaries. “No learned decay” disables learned soft fading, while these eligibility and age limits still apply.

08 · The prediction headHow do the numbers become a valid candle?

A head is the small final network that converts a representation into the output we need. Our price head applies LayerNorm, which rescales the internal representation, followed by a linear layer producing four values.

The decoder interprets them as an opening prediction, a closing prediction, an upper excursion and a lower excursion. The excursions pass through softplus, a function that makes them positive.

High = higher of predicted open and close + positive upper excursion
Low = lower of predicted open and close − positive lower excursion

These calculations happen in log-price coordinates. We then convert them back to prices relative to the observed decision-day close.

The construction guarantees that the predicted high is at least as high as the predicted open and close, and the predicted low is at least as low as both.

A valid candle is not necessarily an accurate candle. The output structure prevents impossible OHLC ordering; training still has to learn useful forecasts.

09 · Learning from mistakesWhat happens in one training step?

A batch is a group of examples processed before a weight update. Our batch contains 16 complete decision dates, each containing all 100 stocks: 1,600 stock examples.

16dates ×100stocks ×30sessions ×19features
  1. 1. Prepare
  2. 2. Predict
  3. 3. Score
  4. 4. Backpropagate
  5. 5. Update

Prepare a historical batch

16 dates × 100 stocks

Gather the 30-session history, eligible events and peer links for each date. Keep the actual next-session OHLC separate as the training target.

Stage 1 of 5

Predict, calculate error, and adjust weights

  1. Forward pass: calculate stock summaries, event attention, peer messages and OHLC predictions.
  2. Loss: compare the predictions with the actual historical next-session prices.
  3. Backpropagation: calculate gradients—how changing each trainable weight would affect the loss.
  4. Gradient clipping: limit the total gradient size to prevent excessively large updates.
  5. Optimizer update: change trainable weights, then continue with another batch.

The default loss averages two groups of squared errors: open/close errors and high/low errors. It uses standardized log-price targets so different output scales are accounted for.

Training loss and report metric differ. Training uses standardized squared error. Checkpoint selection and the headline price report use normalized absolute error, or MAE.
Training settingValueBeginner explanation
OptimizerAdamWUses gradient history to adapt weight updates.
Learning rate0.001Controls the scale of weight updates.
Weight decay0.001Discourages unnecessarily large weights.
Gradient norm limit1.0Rescales gradients if their total norm becomes too large.

The GRU, company/category embeddings, event projection, attention, active routing layers and active prediction heads learn. The cached text embeddings and pretrained text encoder stay fixed. Dropout is not a component of this reference ScaleFinSTAK network.

A little more mathematical detail about the targets

Each price target is log(next-session price / observed decision-day close). Training divides prediction errors by training-fitted target scales before squaring them. The open/close group and high/low group are averaged equally in the default objective. At evaluation, log-ratio predictions are converted back to prices, then absolute error is divided by the observed decision-day close.

10 · Evaluating learningWhen does training stop?

An epoch is one pass through all training examples. Training dates are shuffled between epochs, but the historical sessions inside each example remain in order.

Test foldTrain throughValidation yearTest year
First201920202021
Second202020212022
Third202120222023

Training begins in 2014. In each fold, we fit scalers using training data and exclude examples whose next-session labels cross a split boundary.

  1. Training: update weights on training examples.
  2. Validation: after each epoch, measure OHLC MAE without updating weights.
  3. Checkpoint: save the weights when validation performance improves.
  4. Early stopping: stop after five epochs without sufficient improvement, or at 25 epochs.
  5. Test: evaluate the selected checkpoint on its later historical test period.

Why train three times?

Random starting weights and batch order can change what a network learns. We train each configuration using three random seeds: 7, 19 and 41. Each run has its own validation-selected checkpoint.

We average the three selected models’ price forecasts to form an ensemble. We do not average their weights.

17 configurations × 3 seeds × 3 test folds = 153 training fits. The fresh run repeated the same historical tests; it is reproducibility evidence, not a new untouched test period.

11 · The experimentsWhat other components did we test?

An ablation changes or removes a component to measure its contribution. These are separate model configurations, not a list of techniques all active in the reference model.

TechniqueWhat it does
Price-only GRURemoves the event branch to measure what market history alone can do.
No learned decayUses event attention and correlation peers, without learned soft fading. Eligibility and hard age limits remain.
Exponential decayLearns to reduce event influence as the event ages.
LSTMReplaces the price-only GRU with a different sequence-memory architecture.
T-GCN-style controlPasses correlation-based price information before sequence encoding.
Isolated wick gradientsBlocks high/low loss gradients from changing designated shared features and predicted body bounds. This limits interference with the open/close pathway.
Shared OHLCVAdds a trading-volume prediction head. Price and volume tasks share representations.
Uncertainty weightingLearns relative weights for the training-task losses. It does not by itself produce a calibrated price-confidence interval.
PCGradProjects conflicting task gradients to reduce opposing updates on shared parameters. It does not guarantee that all task conflicts disappear.
Hybrid decayMixes faster exponential fading with slower power-law fading.
Sector routingPasses event contributions through static company–sector–company groups instead of correlation peers, excluding the recipient’s own contribution.
Primary-discloser filterRestricts eligible events using the recorded automated primary-discloser check.
Two-layer GRUStacks a second sequence-processing layer.
Auxiliary direction taskAdds an up/down classification head and classification loss.
FinBERT embeddingsChanges the frozen event-text representation while retaining matched events and routing.
Dual streamsGives valuation and dispersion tasks separate trainable pathways.
Combined recipeTests a 20-session two-layer GRU, FinBERT, hybrid decay, sector routing, primary-discloser filtering, isolated wicks, volume, auxiliary direction and PCGrad together.

Volume-capable variants add a normalized log-volume squared-error task. The auxiliary direction head uses a binary classification loss. PCGrad changes how task gradients are combined; uncertainty weighting changes how their losses are weighted. All configurations still select checkpoints using validation OHLC MAE.

More components did not automatically mean better predictions. No-decay FinSTAK reached 0.964184% pooled OHLC error; the combined recipe reached 0.973310%, where lower is better. Hybrid decay’s tiny observed advantage was inconclusive under the exploratory paired interval.

What is supplied, and what is learned?

Source records, cached text vectors and return correlations are prepared from available data. The forecasting network learns how to use them by repeatedly comparing predictions with historical outcomes.

Implementation sources and scope

This guide describes the restored 100-stock implementation: stak_graph/models/scale_finstak.py, models/finstak.py, experiments/scale_panel.py, experiments/scale_run.py, and embeddings/backend.py, finbert.py and pipeline.py. These reside under the restored study’s frozen source_snapshot.

The scale-study event collection uses rule-based source-evidence extraction, not generative LLM relationship extraction. The architecture is a project-specific recurrent encoder with typed attention, not a full HGT reproduction. Event-source traceability and attention weights do not establish causal explanations. The study does not implement a historical supply-chain network or a live market-data service.

This guide explains the implemented historical research model. All examples are illustrative unless explicitly identified as experiment settings or measured results. The file works offline; diagrams and the training walkthrough require no external services.
